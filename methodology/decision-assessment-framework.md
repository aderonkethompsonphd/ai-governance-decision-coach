# Decision assessment framework

## Objective

The framework helps a learner move from a proposed AI use to a proportionate, defensible governance decision. It is intended for preliminary assessment and professional learning.

## Interaction sequence

1. Present the scenario and available evidence.
2. Require the learner to assess it before revealing a reference analysis.
3. Test purpose, people, data, autonomy, harms, governance exposure, third parties and reversibility.
4. Separate inherent risk from safeguards and operating evidence.
5. Identify uncertainty, evidence gaps and specialist-review triggers.
6. Recommend a decision, conditions and accountable owner.
7. Provide constructive feedback and transferable learning points.

## Eight assessment dimensions

### Purpose and necessity

Define the desired outcome, test whether AI is suitable and compare less intrusive or non-AI alternatives.

### Affected people

Identify who benefits, bears risk, may be excluded or misrepresented and may struggle to understand or challenge an outcome.

### Data

Examine sources, categories, sensitivity, provenance, authorisation, quality, minimisation, access, retention, transfers and deletion.

### Autonomy and human oversight

Determine what the system does and whether people have competence, time, information and authority to intervene, correct, reject, stop and escalate.

### Possible harms

Consider privacy, discrimination, economic, psychological, physical, security, dignity, reputational, operational and systemic harms as relevant to the use.

### Governance and regulatory exposure

Identify organisational policies, management-system requirements, sector duties and plausible legal questions without making unsupported legal conclusions.

### Third-party dependency

Examine vendor claims, external models, subprocessors, contracts, audit rights, change notification, incident support, continuity and exit capability.

### Reversibility

Test whether the organisation can stop the system, correct outputs, restore a manual process, delete or return data and address effects that have already occurred.

## Risk and control reasoning

**Inherent risk** arises from the nature, purpose, scale and context of the use before safeguards receive credit.

**Control design** asks whether a safeguard is capable of addressing the identified risk.

**Operating evidence** demonstrates whether the control is implemented and working in practice.

**Residual risk** is the remaining exposure after the effectiveness of relevant controls has been verified.

**Uncertainty** is stated separately from severity. Material uncertainty may justify a pause, restricted pilot or specialist escalation.

## Evidence labels

- **Verified evidence:** supported by a supplied authoritative document or validated record.
- **User-provided information:** supplied for the exercise but not independently verified.
- **Assumption:** a stated placeholder required to proceed.
- **Missing evidence:** information required before control effectiveness, risk acceptance or approval can be substantiated.

## Escalation triggers

Specialist review should be required or strongly considered when a use affects significant opportunities or rights, involves safety or critical infrastructure, processes sensitive or large-scale personal data, creates difficult-to-contest effects or contains material uncertainty about legality, discriminatory impact, security, oversight, model provenance or vendor claims.

## Required output

Every completed exercise contains:

1. Scenario summary
2. Questions for the user
3. Provisional risk assessment
4. Applicable governance considerations
5. Required controls
6. Evidence gaps
7. Escalation finding
8. Recommended decision
9. What the user assessed well
10. Learning points
